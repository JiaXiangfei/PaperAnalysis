ABSTRACT
The great success of Transformer-based models benefits from the powerful multi-head self-attention mechanism, which learns token dependencies and encodes contextual information from the input. Prior work strives to attribute model decisions to individual input features with different saliency measures, but they fail to explain how these input features interact with each other to reach predictions. In this paper, we propose a self-attention attribution method to interpret the information interactions inside Transformer. We take BERT as an example to conduct extensive studies. Firstly, we apply self-attention attribution to identify the important attention heads, while others can be pruned with marginal performance degradation. Furthermore, we extract the most salient dependencies in each layer to construct an attribution tree, which reveals the hierarchical interactions inside Transformer. Finally, we show that the attribution results can be used as adversarial patterns to implement non-targeted attacks towards BERT.

3METHODS:SELF-ATTENTIONATTRIBUTION
The paper presents a novel framework model, ATTATTR, designed to interpret the inner workings of Transformer-based models, with a focus on BERT. The model addresses the challenge of understanding the dense attention score matrices in Transformers by attributing model decisions to self-attention relations. 

The method used in ATTATTR is inspired by integrated gradients, which manipulates internal attention scores to observe the corresponding model dynamics. The authors calculate an attribution score for each attention connection, taking into account not only the attention scores but also the sensitivity of model predictions to an attention relation. 

One of the innovations of ATTATTR is the use of Riemann approximation to efficiently compute the attribution score. This involves summing the gradients at points along the straight-line path from the zero attention matrix to the original attention weight. 

The model addresses the shortcomings of existing research by demonstrating that attention weights do not always correlate with contributions to model predictions. ATTATTR can prune attention heads with minimal performance loss and expose model vulnerabilities by identifying over-emphasized word patterns that can be used as adversarial triggers to attack BERT models. 

In summary, ATTATTR provides a more robust understanding of Transformer models by interpreting model decisions and uncovering anomalous patterns in data. The method offers a new perspective on the role of attention in Transformers and contributes to the development of more interpretable and secure AI models.

4EXPERIMENTS
In the experiments section of the paper, the authors employ BERT-base-cased to evaluate the effectiveness of their self-attention attribution method, ATTATTR. They fine-tune BERT on four classification datasets: MNLI, RTE, SST-2, and MRPC, using accuracy as the evaluation metric and following the settings suggested by Devlin et al. (2019).

The effectiveness of ATTATTR is demonstrated through quantitative analysis, where the authors prune attention heads based on attribution scores and compare the performance change with a baseline that prunes heads using average attention scores. The results show that ATTATTR better indicates the importance of attention heads, as pruning heads with high attribution scores significantly impacts model accuracy.

The authors also explore attention head pruning, defining the importance of an attention head based on the maximum attribution value with respect to the golden label. They compare their method with other importance metrics and find that ATTATTR is competitive and indicates the importance of interactions within the Transformer.

Furthermore, the paper investigates the universality of important heads across different datasets and tasks, finding that important heads are consistent across datasets with homogeneous tasks, such as RTE, MPRC, and MNLI, which are all about entailment detection. However, the important heads differ for SST-2, which is a sentiment classification task.

The authors propose a heuristic algorithm to construct attribution trees, which visualizes the information flow inside the Transformer and provides insights into the dependencies captured by the model. They demonstrate the construction of attribution trees with examples from MNLI and SST-2, showing how input words interact and contribute to the final prediction.

Additionally, the paper examines the effective receptive field in Transformers, plotting the distance distribution of interactions extracted by the attribution tree. The results indicate that Transformers tend to conduct local encoding and then learn to match between input sentences for paired-input tasks like MNLI.

Lastly, the authors use ATTATTR to identify over-emphasized word patterns as adversarial triggers to attack BERT models. They demonstrate that inserting these triggers into test inputs can significantly reduce model accuracy, exposing model vulnerabilities and the need for a more robust understanding of Transformer models.
5RELATEDWORK
The quest for interpretability in neural networks has led to a diverse range of research efforts, which can be broadly categorized into three areas: saliency measures, hierarchical explanations, and attention mechanism analysis.

Saliency measures, such as DeepLift, layer-wise relevance propagation, and Integrated Gradients, aim to attribute predictions to input features. These methods have been instrumental in understanding the influence of individual components in neural network decisions. However, they often fail to capture the complex interactions between features, which is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of model behavior.

In the natural language processing (NLP) domain, researchers have attempted to track word importance in LSTM models and extend this to contextual decomposition to capture contributions of word combinations. Hierarchical explanations have also been proposed to reveal how features are composed together. However, these methods often focus on interactions within contiguous chunks of input tokens, limiting their ability to capture broader interactions.

The attention mechanism, introduced in Transformer networks, has been another focal point for interpretability research. While attention weights provide an intuitive indication of dependency between words, studies have shown that they do not always offer meaningful explanations for model predictions. Alternative tests have been proposed to assess the usefulness of attention mechanisms, and efforts have been made to interpret intermediate layers of NLI models by visualizing attention and gating signals.

For Transformer networks, specifically BERT, researchers have proposed attention-based visualization methods and probing classifiers to explain model behaviors. However, studies have shown that self-attention distributions are not directly interpretable. Some work has focused on extracting latent syntactic trees from hidden representations and attention weights to provide further insights.

The proposed self-attention attribution method, ATTATTR, addresses the shortcomings of existing research by using integrated gradients to identify important attention connections and construct attribution trees that reveal hierarchical interactions within the Transformer. This approach not only interprets model decisions but also uncovers anomalous patterns in data, contributing to a more robust understanding of Transformer models. By pruning attention heads with minimal performance loss and exposing model vulnerabilities, ATTATTR offers a significant advancement in the field of neural network interpretability.
